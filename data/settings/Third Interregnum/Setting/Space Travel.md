hyperspace
* Sectors
	* contiguous spans of 2-brane spacetime (so effectively a 2d map linking to 3d space)
	* hyperspatial distance measured in 'hexes'
	* sectors touch on the deeper reaches of hyperspace at certain points, "voids"
		* with a J-2 or better drive, you can jump from a sector, cross a void and reach another sector
		* voids touch on multiple sectors, and can touch a given sector at multiple systems (usually systems fairly close together in sector-space), a kind of sector "coast" the void "sea" touches on, with a few landings.
	* 
	* sector "geography"
		* mains: a group of adjacent systems, so a J-1 drive can hop from system to system across the whole main.  some are more linear, some clumps.
			* because you can't jump over a system, they can also represent obstacles to quick travel across a main.
			* a main also assume a certain size / number of systems; not a precise term, but generally at least 4
			* because of their size and accessibility, mains are often the cores of settlement in a sector
		* reaches
			* a notional "archipelago" of systems fairly close to each other, somewhat isolated from other reaches (probably there are only certain systems you can readily enter / leave the reach from)
			* membership in a reach is also partly a matter of traffic and habitation; a system that routinely interacts with others may be considered part of a reach even if it's a bit more distant.
		* gulfs
			* an imprecise term for a space in a sector mostly devoid of systems, and so hard to cross; you need to go around its edge or find a stepper.
		* stepper / stepping-stone / bridge systems
			* systems positioned to allow crossing of a gulf or linking between reaches, but otherwise fairly isolated (not thought of as part of a reach or main)
			* some are bridge to multiple reaches, effectively a crossroads.  some might be part of a reach or main, but act as a bridge between two other reaches (a peninsular used as a stepping-stone for travel tangent to the reach it's in)
	* 
	* "drift"
		* the average direction of energy flow within a sector, gives it a cardinal pair of directions, "driftward" or "downdrift" and "updrift".  Driftward is similar to north, when drawing sector maps.
		* "port" and "starboard" as "west" and "east" relative to driftward's north?
		* "up" and "down" are sometimes used to refer to moving toward realspace or deeper into hyperspace
	* 
	* hyperspace can have "terrain" with various qualities
		* clear: normal passage
		* turbulent: greater risk of jump mishaps
		* fast: the energy flows are quicker; don't count this hex toward distance when making a jump (so a lower rated drive can cross farther)
		* slow: this hex counts as two for jump distances
		* obstructed: you can't cross this hex, treat it as occupied by a system, but there's no destination
		* downwell: only in hexes with system; a link to a specific inter-sector void; you can only enter the void by jumping out from the system in the hex
		* shortcut: effectively a void that only touches on this sector, but at two or more fairly separate spots, allowing one to jump from one area of a sector to another; usually takes normal jump time for a sector, not the month of inter-sector travel
			* you can use the "coastal" systems of a void this way too, but they're usually not very far apart, so this is less of a time savings
		* 
		* obstruction and turbulence can also exist as one or more hex edges, rather than filling hexes, so you could have a wall that blocks jumps or endangers them; if you pass through such an edge, treat it as if the hex had that quality
	* hyperspace "weather"
		* turbulence can occur on a short time scale (days, months), like weather, making a jump route easier (existing turbulence can clear for a time) or harder (turbulent ramps up, perhaps even to the point of obstruction).  this can be random or cyclic, and some regions may be more or less prone to changes in weather.
* Clusters
	* clusters are collections of sectors, node maps where nodes are entire sectors, linked by interconnecting voids
	* transit across a void takes on the order of a month (do faster drives help?)
	* transit happens at stars, same as sector travel, but one jumps "down" into the void, rather than "horizontally" toward another system.
	* clusters don't really have a size beyond the travel time to cross; there is no "hex distance".
		* or maybe some voids might require a J-3 to cross?

Jump Travel
* j-drive rating is the number of hexes it can span.  jumps take about a week.
	* J-1 ships are "coasters", moving around within mains
	* J-2 ships are the common sector-traversing ships, and able to move between sectors across voids
	* J-3 ships are able to make crossings outside of the usual routes within a sector
* jumps use "fuel", a hydrogen plasma used to fill an envelop around the ship that keeps it in a stable bubble of spacetime.  raw fuel is just hydrogen, refined fuel has been cleaned of trace contaminants and doped with certain atoms to improve stability for the jump, "dirty" fuel has more contaminants that can make the envelope less stable (usually scooped from a gas giant without enough time or care spent cleaning to even raw status)
* 
* jump points
	* a j-drive can only transit into hyperspace at jump points.
	* hyperspace is filled with a vast web of energy flows (looks a little like the large scale universe) that ships ride, crisscrossing between them.  this is the "lattice"?
		* the skill of navigating is choosing paths that minimize sharp changes in flow rate/power (turbulence; requiring more fuel and power from your ju-drive and thus wear and tear on the ship) and/or are efficient paths (accounting for flow speed, distance, wasted loop-de-loops, etc)
		* when these flows touch the gravitic shadow of a star in realspace, they adhere, creating jump points.  when you can navigate a route from one star, through the lattice, to another star, you have a jump route.
			* jump points have no physical appearance; they're just soft spot where a j-drive can transit into the lattice.
				* could call them "shoals", a la Collapsing Empire.
			* you can't jump through a hex with a system in it because the lattice adheres to it, so attempting to would drop you at the blocking system, if you make it.
		* locating the jump points within a system and mapping them to a neighbor is a matter of a lot of scouting, measurement taking, dropping probes into the flow and mapping their trajectories, and heavy, heavy math.  usually done by experts long ago and passed down in commercial charts.
			* some jump points have just never been charted or promulgated.  others may have been charted but kept secret
		* being gravitationally bound, jump points orbit the star, and can even become caught in orbit of planets (usually gas giants).  they can follow elliptical orbits, be inside the outer skin of a star, be far out in the outer system, etc.
			* waystations and defensive platforms can be built to orbit along with a jump point.
			* jump points can be anywhere in a system, so some might be hard to use, being dangerously close to the sun or far in the outer system, requiring long travel to reach.  Most are within about 0.5 to 10 AU.
		* more powerful j-drives can handle more powerful energy flow (often diving "deeper" to reach them).  after about a week in hyperspace, the fuel envelope starts getting tattered, so you need to get where you're going before then.  thus riding higher energy flows means going farther, thus higher rated j-drives having greater reach.
			* misjumps can leave you spending extra time in jump space, leading to envelope degredation and ship damage or crew exposure to Weird Shit.
			* the envelope can persist for eons in some cases, so you can have ancient derelicts finally dumped out of hyperspace, but they'll have been marinating in Weird Shit for a long time; exotic radiation, spatial or temporal distortions, metadimensional (psychic) residue, damaged systems and superstructure, "passengers", psi-ghosts of dead crew, etc.
	* jump points exist for a given system and all other systems, but they become increasingly "muddled" by distance and the presence of all the other systems.  
		* longer range jump points, being "muddled" are harder to access, thus the need for more powerful j-drives


* a rutter is a collections of data on a specific hyperspace route, covering its "terrain", "weather", and energy flows to assist the navigator in a smooth journey.
	* rutters gradually go stale as time passes, so ships usually share their sensor readings and experiences on arrival to help keep rutters up to date.
	* secret routes, routes with little traffic, or newly discovered ancient routes may have very stale rutters, making traversal more dangerous
* jump paths are straight; count all hexes the line passes through (corners don't count, edges count as one hex) to determine its distance.
	* can't chart a jump that crosses a hex occupied by a system.  draw a line from your hex center to the destination, it can touch an occupied hex at an edge or corner, but not cross its interior.

* starbursting for escape?
	*  if you jump randomly into the lattice around a star, not at a jump point, you're likely to just coil back and return to the originating system
	* this can be used for uncontrolled short-range jump that can allow escape from a bad situation.
	* you'll often return worse for the wear since you generally have no info about the energy flows you're about to encounter, or how long it'll take to swing back around, and if eats the same amount of fuel as a proper jump.
