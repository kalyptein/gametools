## Kunlun

- mountain source of four rivers flowing to the four quarters.  home of taoist immortals.  mountain is hollow / full of caves.  mysterious, grand, and magnificent, Kunlun mountain reaches high into the heavens and also deep into the earth

* mount meru / mount sumeru

- gem-like rocks and towering cliffs of jasper and jade, exotic jeweled plants, bizarrely formed and colored magical fungi, and numerous birds and other animals, together with humans who have become immortal beings. Sometimes, it is the Eight Immortals who are seen, coming to pay their respects to the goddess Xiwangmu, perhaps invited to join her in a feast of immortal repast.

- a palace protected by golden ramparts, within which immortals (xian) feasted on bear paws, monkey lips, and the livers of dragons, served at the edge of the Lake of Gems. Every 6,000 years, the peaches that conferred immortality upon those who ate them would be served (except during the time when they were purloined by Monkey King). Originally a plague deity with tiger teeth and leopard tail, Xiwangmu became a beautiful and well-mannered goddess, responsible for guarding the herb of immortality.

- Kunlun is believed to be the representation of the Supreme Deity (Taidi). According to some sources, his throne is at the top tier of the mountain, known as the "Palace of Heaven." As Kunlun was sometimes viewed as the pillar holding up the sky and keeping it separated from the terrestrial plane, some accounts place the top of Kunlun in Heaven rather than locating it on Earth; in this case, the Supreme Deity's abode on Kunlun is actually in Heaven, and Kunlun functions as a sort of ladder that could be used to travel between Earth and Heaven. Accordingly, any person who succeeded in climbing up to the top of Kunlun would magically become an immortal spirit.[15]

- Yu Shi — a Chinese spirit or god of rain, also known as the "Lord of Rain" or "Leader of Rain" — is thought to have his dwelling place upon the Kunlun slopes. During the reign of Shennong, a certain Chisongzi (Master Red Pine) performed a rain-making ceremony that successfully ended a terrible drought, leading to his promotion to "Yu Shi," "Master of Rain."[3]

- According to the Shanhaijing, the top of Kunlun is the habitation of shamans; Wu Peng is depicted holding the herb of immortality there, in the company of five other shamans called Siva Harish.[17]

- Kunlun has a lively bestiary, with various types of more-or-less fantastic beasts and birds present in its environs. Often the tiger or beings with tiger-like features are associated with Kunlun, since the tiger is symbolic of the west, and Kunlun is often associated with the Western Paradise.[18] Creatures symbolic of immortality are often seen or described in depictions of Kunlun, such as deer or cranes. Xiwangmu is often identified as having a spotted deer as a pet. Besides the cranes (traditionally thought of as the mounts or the transformations of immortals), other birds come and go from the mountain, flying errands for Xiwangmu; these blue (or green) birds are her qingniao. Sometimes the poets claim to have received joyful inspiration during a visit by one of these birds, carrying a message from Xiwangmu.

-The flora of Kunlun and its environs are in keeping with the rest of its natural (and supernatural) qualities, including the Pearl and Jade Trees, the Tree of Immortality, and Tree Grain (i.e., Muhe, which was forty feet in height and five spans in thickness).[14] Peaches are (and have been) often associated with Xiwangmu.[19] The langgan was a tree of fairy gems in colours of blue or green, which was reported to grow on Kunlun in the classic books of the Zhou and early Han dynasties.[20]

- Kunlun is described as having various structures, areas, or significant features either on or around the area of the mountain. The palace of Xiwangmu, sometimes described as having golden ramparts, was located on Kunlun; those blessed to gather there might partake of the fruit of longevity.[21] Often, her palace is described as having a park or garden, bordering a Jasper Pool. Of gardens, a (the) Hanging Garden was referenced early on.

- Four rivers were sometimes said to flow out of Kunlun Mountain: the Red River (Chishui, 赤水), the Yellow River, the Black River (黑水, black water), and the Yang River(洋水) (Yang 2005: 161). A fifth river was said to flow around the base of Kunlun, which rose in a way that was particularly steep and hard to climb. This Weak River at the base of Kunlun flowed with a liquid so lacking in density that not even a feather could float upon it. This was a major obstacle, since it could neither be swum or floated over on a vessel (Yang 2005: 162, 219). However, this was an obstacle routinely overcome by those practiced in the way of magic (Daoist or shamanic). Two examples of those who overcame these hindrances were Sun Wukong (Journey to the West) and Qu Yuan in his poem ("Li Sao") — both already on the path to immortality, one as a god and the other as a poet. Another barrier to Kunlun was the dangerous and difficult-to-cross Moving Sands, also known as Flowing Sands or Liusha. According to Shanhaijing (Chapter 16), Kunlun was located south of the West Sea, behind the Red River, and on the shore of Liusha (Yang 2005: 162, 219).

Aziza are benevolent, small fairy-like spirits in West African mythology (specifically Dahomey/Fon folklore) that inhabit forests, anthills, and silk-cotton trees. Known as "little people," they are revered for teaching humans herbal medicine, fire-making, and granting good fortune or protection to respectful hunters. 

Key Aspects of Aziza Mythology:
Guardians of Nature: The Aziza are associated with forest wisdom and are sometimes considered intermediaries between the natural and supernatural worlds.
Benefactors to Humanity: Legends state they taught early humans essential skills and provided spiritual insight.
Appearance: They are often described as tiny creatures, sometimes with butterfly-like wings, creating a "radiant luminescence".
Forest Dwellers: They prefer to remain hidden, appearing only to those who show reverence for nature.
Good Fortune: Hunters and travelers who gain their favor receive guidance, success, and protection. 

They are often considered the African equivalent of European elves or fairies. 

Often described as tiny, hairy humanoids. In some traditions, such as the Jeje, a single divinity named Aziza is depicted as a small, one-legged man smoking a pipe.

Urhobo (Nigeria): Among the Urhobo, Aziza is regarded as a god of ambition and hunting, sometimes associated with the winds or "whirlwinds".

Fon/Dahomey: They are highly revered as ancient spirits who can be invoked for protection and luck

The Aziza do not have a single named city or mythical kingdom like "Camelot" or "Asgard." Instead, their homes are specific features within the deep forest where they live communally in village-like settlements sometimes called Azizan.

Sacred Groves: They are believed to inhabit secluded, wooded areas or "sacred groves" where they act as guardians of nature.

Yumboes (Senegal)
The Yumboes (or Bakhna Rakhna) are benevolent, fairy-like spirits from Wolof folklore. They are often described as the spirits of the dead and are famously known for their hospitality. 

Appearance: They are roughly two feet tall with pearly-white skin and silver hair that glows under the moon.
Lifestyle: They live in magnificent underground dwellings beneath the Paps hills. At night, they come out to dance and feast on large tables, served by invisible servants whose hands and feet are the only parts visible.
Behavior: They are generally helpful and may invite humans to their feasts. However, they are known to steal corn and fire from human villages, though they only take exactly what they need. 

Abatwa (Southern Africa)
The Abatwa are tiny humanoid hunters from Zulu mythology. Unlike the Aziza or Yumboes, they can be quite dangerous if offended. 
Appearance: They are so small they can hide behind a blade of grass or sleep in anthills.
Deadly Etiquette: The Abatwa are extremely sensitive about their size. If you encounter one, it will ask, "From where did you first see me?" You must answer that you saw them from a mountain or far away to imply they are large. Saying you only just saw them is an insult punishable by death with their poisoned arrows.
Sightings: According to legend, they are usually only visible to children under four, pregnant women, and powerful magicians

- Loviatar (likely alter-ego for Louhi) is a blind daughter of Tuoni, the god of death in Finnish mythology and his spouse Tuonetar, the queen of the underworld. Loviatar is regarded as a goddess of death and disease.[1] In Runo 45 of the Kalevala,[2] Loviatar is impregnated by a great wind and gives birth to nine sons, the Nine diseases. In other folk songs, she gives birth to a tenth child, who is a girl.[3]

- Other Kalevala characters also seek marriage with the daughters of Pohjola. These include the adventurer Lemminkäinen, and the wise old man Väinämöinen. Louhi demands from them deeds similar to the forging of Sampo, such as shooting the Swan of Tuonela. When the suitor finally gets the daughter, weddings and great drinking and eating parties are held at the great hall of Pohjola.

Prester John's capital was Ulna, a city in India that Prester John described as the "head and mistress of the Indian kingdom". The city was so large that it took four days to walk around its walls, which were wide enough for two chariots to ride side by side. Prester John also described the Phison, a river of Paradise, that watered the city. Prester John told Pope Calixtus II that he was the temporal and spiritual ruler of the city, and that the body of St Thomas was preserved there. 

Prester John is a legendary Christian ruler of the East who has been part of European mythology for centuries. He is the subject of many letters and artistic works from the medieval period. Prester John's fictional empire was first inferred in India, then in Central Asia, and finally in Africa. The kingdom's location was so flexible due to Europe's poor understanding of geography at the time, and the elasticity of the concept of "India", which could stretch from Africa to China. 

Prester John became a pillar of hope for Christians during the Crusades. His kingdom was believed to be a Christian utopia, and Portuguese explorers sought to gain the kingdom's allegiance to aid in the conversion of Muslims in Africa to Christianity. This search for Prester John's kingdom advanced European exploration of Asia and Africa.


Oponskoye Kingdom
* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oponskoye_Kingdom
* In Russian folklore the Oponskoye Kingdom (Russian: Опонское/опоньское царство, romanized: Oponskoye/opon'skoe tsarstvo, supposed to mean Yaponskoye tsarstvo, or "kingdom of Japan"), or as some English-language sources have it, Kingdom of Opona, is a mythical kingdom, envisioned by Russian peasants as lying at the edge of the flat Earth. Here, they believed, the peasants lived happy lives undisturbed by the state or the gentry, under a "White Tsar" who ruled truly and justly. Other names in legend include the Golden Land the Land of Chud and Belovodye (russian: "Land of White Water").

Hara Berezaiti
* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hara_Berezaiti
* Hara Berezaiti (Avestan: 𐬵𐬀𐬭𐬁⸱𐬠𐬆𐬭𐬆𐬰𐬀𐬌𐬙𐬍, lit. 'High Watch') is a mythical mountain or mountain range in Zoroastrian tradition. Over time, it has been associated with a number of real-world mountains, more specifically Alborz mountain range in Iran.
* In the Avesta, Mount Hara is the home of Mithra. In later texts like the Bundahishn, it appears as a center of the world around which the stars and planets revolve. It is also the gateway to the afterlife. In Iranian legend, it was on Hara Berezaiti that the hero Fereydun fettered Zahhak. Modern version of its name appear in many mountains and mountain ranges over the Iranian world.

The Alatyr
* The Alatyr is a sacred stone in Russian folklore.[1][2] It is the "father to all stones", the navel of the earth, containing sacred letters and endowed with healing properties.[1] Although the name Alatyr appears only in East Slavic sources, the awareness of the existence of such a stone exists in various parts of the Slavdom. It is often mentioned in stories and referred to in love spells as "a mighty force that has no end."
* In the Dove Book, the Alatyr is associated with an altar located in the "navel of the world", in the middle of the world ocean, on the Buyan island. On it stands the World tree. The stone is endowed with healing and magical properties. Spiritual verses describe how "from under the white-alatyr-stone" flows a miraculous source that gives the whole world "food and healing." The Alatyr is guarded by the wise snake Garafena and the bird Gagana.[3]
	* Gagana is a miraculous bird with an iron beak and copper claws featured in Russian folklore.[1][2] She is said to live on the Buyan Island. The bird is often mentioned in incantations. It is also said this bird guards the Alatyr, alongside Garafena the snake.

Buyan
* In Russian folklore, Buyan (Russian: Буя́н), sometimes transliterated as Bujan,[1] is a mysterious island in the ocean with the ability to appear and disappear with the tide. The island is found in byliny and skazki.[2] It gained wider recognition after appearing in Alexander Pushkin's The Tale of Tsar Saltan.
* Buyan is an oceanic island, sometimes described as a paradise.[3] The island of Buyan features in many fairy tales; Koshchei the Deathless keeps his soul of immortality hidden there, secreted inside a needle placed inside an egg in the mystical oak-tree; other legends call the island the source of all weather, generated there and sent forth into the world by the god Perun. Buyan also appears in Alexander Pushkin's Tale of Tsar Saltan.
* It is mentioned in the medieval Dove Book as the place of the mythical stone with healing and magic powers, known as the Alatyr (Russian: Алатырь), which is guarded by the bird Gagana and by Garafena the serpent.[4]
